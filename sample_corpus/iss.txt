The International Space Station

The International Space Station, usually called the ISS, is a large research laboratory that orbits Earth about 400 kilometers above the surface. It travels at roughly 28,000 kilometers per hour and circles the planet once every 90 minutes, so the crew sees about sixteen sunrises and sunsets every day. The station is about the size of an American football field and is the largest structure humans have ever built in space.

Construction began in November 1998 when Russia launched the first module, Zarya, followed weeks later by the American module Unity. The station has been continuously occupied since November 2, 2000, when the first long-term crew arrived. A typical crew has seven people who stay for about six months. Astronauts and cosmonauts arrive on Russian Soyuz capsules and on American commercial spacecraft such as SpaceX Crew Dragon.

The ISS is a partnership between five space agencies: NASA in the United States, Roscosmos in Russia, the European Space Agency, the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency, and the Canadian Space Agency. Its main purpose is to study how living things and materials behave in microgravity, the near weightless state of free fall. Research on board covers protein crystal growth, flame behavior, fluid physics, plant growth and the effects of long spaceflight on the human body, including bone and muscle loss.

Because the station slowly loses altitude from faint atmospheric drag, visiting cargo ships periodically fire their engines to push it higher. The partners plan to keep the ISS operating until about 2030, after which it is to be guided into a controlled reentry over a remote part of the Pacific Ocean.
