The Early Space Race

The space age began on October 4, 1957, when the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, a polished metal sphere of 83.6 kilograms with four radio antennas. Its steady beeping could be heard by amateur radio operators around the world, and it orbited Earth for about three months. The launch shocked the United States and started a technological competition between the two superpowers. A month later Sputnik 2 carried a dog named Laika into orbit, the first animal to circle the Earth.

The United States answered with Explorer 1 on January 31, 1958, which discovered the Van Allen radiation belts. NASA was founded later that year. The Soviets kept scoring firsts. On April 12, 1961, cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin flew aboard Vostok 1 and became the first human in space, completing one orbit of Earth in 108 minutes. Less than a month later, on May 5, Alan Shepard became the first American in space, on a fifteen minute suborbital flight. John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth on February 20, 1962, in the capsule Friendship 7. In 1963 Valentina Tereshkova became the first woman in space, and in March 1965 Alexei Leonov performed the first spacewalk.

On May 25, 1961, President John F. Kennedy told Congress that the United States should commit itself to landing a man on the Moon and returning him safely before the decade was out. The Gemini missions then practiced the skills needed, such as docking two spacecraft and working outside the capsule. Tragedy struck on January 27, 1967, when a fire during a ground test of Apollo 1 killed astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee. The spacecraft was redesigned, and the program recovered to reach the Moon in 1969.
