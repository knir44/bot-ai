The Hubble Space Telescope

The Hubble Space Telescope was launched on April 24, 1990, aboard the Space Shuttle Discovery, and it still operates today. It circles Earth at an altitude of about 540 kilometers, above the blurring effect of the atmosphere, and carries a 2.4 meter mirror. Hubble observes visible light, ultraviolet light and some near-infrared light. It is named after the astronomer Edwin Hubble, who showed in the 1920s that the universe contains many galaxies beyond the Milky Way and that they are moving apart.

Shortly after launch, astronomers discovered that Hubble's main mirror had been ground to the wrong shape, a flaw called spherical aberration, so the first images came out blurry. In December 1993 shuttle astronauts performed a servicing mission and installed corrective optics, including a package named COSTAR, like a pair of eyeglasses for the telescope. The repair was a triumph, and images became razor sharp. Astronauts visited Hubble five times in total, replacing instruments, gyroscopes and batteries. The last servicing mission was in 2009.

Hubble's discoveries have reshaped astronomy. The Hubble Deep Field of 1995 pointed the telescope at an apparently empty patch of sky for ten days and revealed thousands of distant galaxies. Measurements of pulsating stars and exploding stars helped fix the age of the universe at about 13.8 billion years and contributed to the discovery that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, driven by what is called dark energy. Hubble has also photographed the aurora on Jupiter, the Pillars of Creation in the Eagle Nebula, and the atmospheres of exoplanets.
